01 / THE ROADMAP

Learn one skill. Make one thing.

Start with a short, achievable project: a 15–30 second montage made from your own footage. Your first goal is a clear beginning, middle and ending. Add a simple title and clean sound before experimenting with complex effects.

01Build the edit

Import, organize, trim, arrange and tell a small story.

02Give it movement

Animate a title, shape the timing and refine the sound.

03Finish & share

Match the color, export, watch the result and revise.

Watch a lesson once to understand the idea. Follow it with your own footage, then repeat the technique without the video. Keep a note of what worked and what you would change. The milestones below are a practice checklist, not a certification.

02 / GET SET UP

Build a workspace that works for you.

Check your editor’s current requirements before installing or buying hardware. There is no single PC specification that suits every codec, resolution, effect or workload. Begin with short 1080p projects, reduce preview resolution when needed, and measure the actual bottleneck before upgrading.

  • Storage: keep free space for media, cache and exports. Use a reliable SSD where available; cache is temporary, your footage is not.
  • Memory & graphics: follow the requirements for the exact application version. A supported GPU and driver matter more than a model name in an old guide.
  • Organization: create folders for Footage, Audio, Graphics, Projects and Exports. Use filenames you can recognize.
  • Recovery: turn on auto-save, keep numbered project versions, and store a second copy of your project and original media on another drive.
Check Adobe’s current After Effects requirements ↗
03 / CHOOSE YOUR TOOLS

Editing and motion graphics are different jobs.

Premiere

A timeline workflow for cutting footage, dialogue, music, titles and longer sequences.

After Effects

Layer-based animation, compositing, masks, tracking and visual effects. Build shots and graphics here.

DaVinci Resolve

Editing, color, audio and Fusion compositing in one application. Blackmagic provides free training.

Pick one editing application first. Use a compatible, stable release and check support for your footage and plugins. Do not change versions in the middle of an important project without saving an independent copy. Newer projects may require newer application versions; test compatibility before collaborating.

Follow the interface concepts even if a tutorial uses an older version. Buttons and panel locations can change. Use the application’s help when a step differs.

04 / YOUR FIRST PROJECT

A first edit, from import to rough cut.

  1. Plan: choose a subject, the intended audience, the platform and the aspect ratio. Write one sentence describing what the viewer should feel or understand.
  2. Import: copy original footage into your project folder, import it, and organize bins. Watch your clips before choosing them.
  3. Create the timeline: use a sequence or composition suited to the delivery. Inspect source frame rate and dimensions rather than guessing.
  4. Make selects: mark usable moments, trim unnecessary lead-in and out, then arrange five to ten shots into a rough story.
  5. Refine: remove repeated information. Check what each cut adds. Use straight cuts until the sequence works without transitions.
  6. Add sound and one title: balance audio and keep the title readable. Save a new version before polishing.
  7. Preview: play the whole sequence without stopping. Make notes and correct the biggest issue first.
Try it: make a 20-second edit with at least five clips, one title and a beginning/middle/end. Use footage you created or have permission to use.
05 / SET THE FRAME

Resolution, aspect ratio and frame rate.

Choose settings based on the delivery and the source. Landscape 1920 × 1080 and portrait 1080 × 1920 are useful practice formats. Check the actual destination requirements for a real delivery.

  • Frame rate: 23.976, 24, 25, 29.97, 30, 50 and 60 are distinct formats. Choose deliberately; a higher number does not automatically improve every edit.
  • Slow motion: high-frame-rate footage can be played more slowly in a lower-frame-rate timeline. Interpolation can create artifacts; inspect fast motion and edges.
  • Mixed sources: expect some footage to be converted to the timeline rate. Look for duplicated frames or uneven movement before export.
  • Motion blur: use it when appropriate for movement. It does not repair a mismatched frame rate.
  • Stylized stepping: apply a frame-stepping effect selectively when you want a stop-motion look, rather than changing the entire project by accident.
06 / MAKE IT MOVE

Keyframes, easing and visual hierarchy.

A keyframe stores a property value at a time. Two keyframes define a change; interpolation controls what happens between them. Begin with position and opacity before stacking many effects.

  1. Create a text or shape layer and set its starting position and opacity.
  2. Enable animation with the property’s stopwatch, move forward in time, and set the ending values.
  3. Compare linear movement with eased movement. In After Effects, try Easy Ease and inspect the Graph Editor.
  4. Adjust timing first, then the curve. Avoid extreme overshoots that distract from the subject.
  5. Use parenting to move related layers together; precompose when it makes the layer structure easier to manage.
  6. Add masks or mattes for a controlled reveal. Preview at the final size to check edges.
Try it: animate a title entering over half a second, holding long enough to read, then leaving. Make a second version with different easing and explain which feels better.

Tracking follows movement in footage; rotoscoping separates a subject. Learn these after basic keyframes, and inspect the result across the entire shot. Expressions can automate properties, but are optional for your first projects.

07 / SOUND & RHYTHM

The edit should work with your eyes closed.

  • Dialogue first: make speech intelligible, then bring music underneath it. Reduce music during speech rather than raising everything together.
  • Avoid clipping: watch the meters and listen for distortion. Do not normalize every clip blindly; use the delivery’s loudness specification when one is provided.
  • Build continuity: room tone, ambience and short fades can soften abrupt audio cuts.
  • Cut with intent: beats are useful markers, but not every cut must land on a beat. Let action, emotion and story guide the rhythm.
  • Check more than headphones: listen on ordinary speakers too. Quiet and loud sections should remain understandable.
  • Use permitted music: check the license for your platform and usage. Attribution is not always sufficient.
Try it: compare your montage with and without sound. Add three restrained sound details that support what the viewer sees.
08 / THE FINISH

Match the shots before creating a look.

Color correction fixes exposure, white balance and consistency. A creative grade adds a deliberate mood. Work in that order and use scopes alongside your eyes.

  • Check the project’s color management and the source color space, especially for log or HDR footage. Avoid applying multiple conflicting conversions.
  • Match neighboring shots before adding contrast or saturation. Watch skin tones and bright areas for unwanted shifts.
  • A LUT is a transformation, not a universal repair. Use it only with the input it expects, and adjust the result.
  • Design titles with strong contrast, readable size and enough screen time. Keep important text away from interface overlays at the destination.
  • Add captions when useful and proofread names, numbers and timing. Watch on a phone before delivering a vertical edit.
Try it: match three shots, create one restrained look, and keep a before/after version for comparison.
09 / EFFECTS WITH PURPOSE

Start with the tools you already have.

Native transforms, masks, blur, glow, color controls and text animation are enough for the practice projects. Add a plugin only when it solves a specific problem. Check application version, platform, license and the developer’s installation instructions.

Motion blur & retiming
Tools such as RSMB and Twixtor can help specific shots. Check interpolation artifacts.
Color & looks
Magic Bullet Looks and similar tools support creative finishing after shot matching.
Glows & light
Saber, Deep Glow and Optical Flares are optional ways to build stylized light.
Effect collections
Sapphire, Continuum and Universe offer many tools. Learn one effect at a time.
Particles & 3D
Trapcode and Element 3D cover specialized tasks after you understand compositions.
Stylized movement
Effects such as Twitch are finishing choices; clear timing should come first.

Test unfamiliar tools in a copy of a project. Keep notes of dependencies when sharing a project with another editor.

10 / REUSABLE ANIMATION

Save a preset you understand.

In After Effects, select the properties you want to reuse, then choose Animation → Save Animation Preset. A preset can contain effects and animated properties; inspect what it changes after applying it.

  1. Create a short test animation and select its relevant properties.
  2. Save the .ffx preset in your current version’s user presets location.
  3. Apply it to a new test layer at the intended time. Check scale, anchor point, timing and any effect dependencies.
  4. Name the preset clearly and document the version and intended use.

Folder names vary by installed version. Use the application’s preset browser or help rather than assuming a hard-coded “2024” path. Do not overwrite application defaults.

11 / CREATIVE LOOKS

Looks, presets and LUTs are not interchangeable.

Magic Bullet Looks presets, animation presets and LUTs serve different purposes. A file extension alone does not guarantee compatibility with your installed version.

For .mblook or .rgx files, check the installed product’s preset importer and vendor documentation. Avoid copying files into an old system folder just because a tutorial used that path. Apply the look to a duplicate adjustment layer, compare before/after and reduce its strength if needed.

Keep your original color conversion separate from the creative look. If skin tones, shadows or highlights break, revisit the input color space and exposure first.

12 / EXPORT & REVIEW

Your export is part of the edit.

  1. Confirm the intended range, dimensions, frame rate, audio tracks and color settings.
  2. Use an appropriate delivery preset. MP4 with H.264 is a common sharing format; a higher-quality intermediate may be appropriate for a master or further editing.
  3. For YouTube, consult its current encoding guidance instead of treating one bitrate as correct for every resolution and frame rate.
  4. Export a short test containing movement, a title and audio before rendering the full project.
  5. Watch the exported file from start to finish. Check black frames, missing media, spelling, timing, color and sound.
  6. Keep the project and original media, plus a clearly named final export. A compressed upload is not your only backup.
YouTube’s current upload encoding guidance ↗
Delivery checklist: correct range · correct format · clean audio · readable titles · no missing media · full playback checked.

16 / WHEN SOMETHING BREAKS

Change one thing at a time.

Preview is slow or stutters

Reduce preview resolution, use a shorter work area, let frames cache, and temporarily disable heavy effects. Consider proxies for demanding media. Check free cache space and close unneeded applications before assuming the final export will stutter.

Media is offline

Relink the missing files and verify their location. If you moved the project, include the original media and any external assets. Avoid renaming source files while a project depends on them.

A plugin or preset does not work

Check the supported host version, platform and dependencies. Try a clean test project with a native effect, then add the third-party tool. Use the developer’s installation and troubleshooting guidance.

The export looks wrong

Compare source, project and export color settings. Check the chosen range, dimensions and frame rate. Test in a reliable player and compare another display; player and display differences can affect appearance.

The program crashes while rendering

Save a copy, note the error and isolate the failing time range. Test with demanding effects disabled, check available storage and supported drivers, and consult the editor’s official support. Keep the working version available before updating.

The effect looks good, but the edit feels weak

Remove the effects and revisit the selects, story and pacing. Ask what each shot contributes. Watch once without sound and once listening only, then repair the weakest part before adding polish.

17 / KEEP THIS HANDY

The words you will use every day.

Sequence / composition
The timeline or canvas where you arrange and combine your work.
Codec / container
A codec encodes media; a container holds it. An .mp4 extension alone does not describe every encoding detail.
Keyframe / easing
A saved property value at a time, and the way movement speeds up or slows down between values.
Proxy
A lighter working copy of demanding source media, used to make editing more responsive.
Mask / matte
Ways to control which parts of an image or layer are visible or affected.
Precomposition
A composition nested inside another composition to organize or combine layers.
LUT / grade
A color transformation table, and the broader creative process of shaping the look of your footage.
Render / export
Calculating the result and encoding a deliverable file. Always inspect that file afterward.
